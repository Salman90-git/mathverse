The Mandelbrot Set is defined on the complex plane. For each point c = a + bi, we iterate the map
z₀ = 0, zₙ₊₁ = zₙ² + c
A point belongs to the set if the sequence |zₙ| never exceeds 2 (it remains bounded forever). The boundary between bounded and unbounded points forms an infinitely intricate fractal. Zooming in reveals ever-finer self-similar structures — seahorse valleys, elephant valleys, and mini-brots — each hiding further complexity no matter how deep you go. Colors represent how quickly a point escapes, producing smooth gradient halos around the set's edge.

The Sierpiński Triangle is a classic example of a self-similar fractal. At each recursion step, the triangle is divided into four smaller congruent triangles and the central one is removed — leaving three copies at half scale.
Fractal dimension = log(3) / log(2) ≈ 1.585
This dimension, between 1 and 2, captures the idea that the Sierpiński triangle is "more than a line but less than a filled area." After infinite recursion the total area is 0, yet the perimeter is infinite — a hallmark of fractal geometry. The same pattern emerges in Pascal's triangle (odd entries), cellular automata Rule 90, and many natural phenomena.
